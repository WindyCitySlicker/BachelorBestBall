Kohler, Wisconsin · Golf heritage

Course History

From a bold resort vision to championship golf on the shores of Lake Michigan.

First things first · The Kohler sheep

The woolly locals of Whistling Straits

Those Scottish Blackface sheep grazing along Whistling Straits are more than a photo opportunity: they are part of the course’s links-inspired identity. Herb Kohler brought the flock to help evoke the feel of traditional golf landscapes in Ireland and Scotland. Their presence is a memorable reminder that this dramatic Lake Michigan shoreline was designed to feel like a rugged seaside links, not a manicured parkland course.

Sheep fact: the Straits course was built on a former U.S. Army anti-aircraft training facility and later landfill property. The transformation involved major earthmoving and imported sand to create its dunes, bluffs, and links-style contours.

How Kohler became a golf destination

The story began with resort guests asking for better golf options. Herb Kohler and legendary architect Pete Dye turned that demand into a long-term vision for championship golf in Wisconsin.

Kohler Co. is founded by John Michael Kohler.
1981
The American Club reopens as a resort hotel, helping plant the seed for a golf destination.
1988
Blackwolf Run opens and quickly earns national attention.
1998
Whistling Straits’ Straits course opens along Lake Michigan.
2000
The Irish course opens, completing Whistling Straits’ 36-hole offering.

Whistling Straits

Two Pete Dye-designed courses sit beside Lake Michigan, each with a distinct identity and a strong links influence.

  • The Straits: exposed, rugged shoreline golf with dramatic views and deep bunkering.
  • The Irish: a links-inspired mix of dunes, inland terrain, and holes shaped by Seven Mile Creek.

The Straits has hosted the PGA Championship in 2004, 2010, and 2015, plus the 2021 Ryder Cup. The Irish course opened in 2000.

Official Whistling Straits history & course details ↗

Blackwolf Run

Opened in 1988, Blackwolf Run brought Pete Dye and Herb Kohler’s vision to the glacially carved Sheboygan River Valley. Its terrain contrasts with Whistling Straits’ shoreline links.

  • River: dramatic valley and bluff terrain following the river corridor.
  • Meadow Valleys: rolling prairie, oak savannah, and ravines.
  • The Baths: a 10-hole par-3 course with a two-acre putting course, opened in 2021.

Blackwolf Run hosted the U.S. Women’s Open in 1998 and 2012.

Championship moments

Kohler’s courses have welcomed major championships and international team golf, putting this stretch of Wisconsin on the world golf map.

  • 1998: Se Ri Pak wins the U.S. Women’s Open at Blackwolf Run.
  • 2004: Vijay Singh wins the PGA Championship at Whistling Straits.
  • 2010: Martin Kaymer wins the PGA Championship.
  • 2012: Na Yeon Choi wins the U.S. Women’s Open at Blackwolf Run.
  • 2015: Jason Day wins the PGA Championship.
  • 2021: Europe wins the Ryder Cup at Whistling Straits.

What is fescue?

Fescues are a large group of cool-season grasses in the genus Festuca. Depending on the species, they can form fine, dense turf or grow in taller, tougher clumps. On links-style courses, longer fescue adds texture, frames holes, catches errant shots, and moves dramatically in the wind. It can hide a ball below the grass canopy even when you are standing close to it.

Find your golf ball in fescue

  1. Freeze the landing spot. As soon as the ball comes down, pick a fixed landmark behind it—a tree, bunker edge, sign, or roofline. Walk toward that line instead of searching at random.
  2. Start at the last-seen point. Search the likely landing area first, then widen the pattern in short lanes. Have partners cover separate strips rather than all walking over the same ground.
  3. Get low and use the light. Crouch and scan from several angles. A white ball may show as a small bright patch or dimple between stems; gently part the grass with your hand or club.
  4. Move slowly; don’t stomp. Take short steps and look before placing each foot. Avoid kicking or sweeping the ball away, and don’t trample a large circle that makes the area harder to search.
  5. Know the clock. Under the Rules of Golf, the search time is three minutes from when you or your caddie begins searching. If the ball may be lost or out of bounds, play a provisional ball when permitted to save a long walk back. USGA Rule 18 ↗
Ford’s field trick

Don’t look for the whole ball at first—look for a curved white edge, a dimple, or a shadow that doesn’t match the grass. Search the base of the stalks, not just the tops.

Fescue care: how to keep it healthy

  • Match the species to the site. Fine fescues generally suit cooler climates and lower-input turf areas; the right blend depends on sun, soil, traffic, and the intended height.
  • Mow to purpose. Keep lawn-type fescue at a suitable, consistent height and avoid scalping. Naturalized or course rough should be managed to its intended height and habitat role—not treated like a putting surface.
  • Water deeply, not constantly. Water established turf when needed and encourage deeper rooting; avoid frequent shallow watering. Newly seeded areas need more consistent moisture while establishing.
  • Feed lightly and test first. Use a soil test to guide pH and fertilizer. Fine fescues often need less nitrogen than high-input turf; excessive fertilizer can encourage lush growth and increase maintenance.
  • Reduce stress. Limit unnecessary traffic when soils are wet, sharpen mower blades, and overseed thin patches with a suitable species or blend during the locally recommended establishment window.
  • Protect natural rough. On a golf course, follow the superintendent’s plan. Avoid blanket mowing, watering, or fertilizing wild rough: species, wildlife, erosion, and course strategy all matter.
